\section{Abstract}\label{abstract}

\textbf{BACKGROUND:} Preventive control of grapevine downy mildew requires
infection risk to be assessed from inoculum availability, host
susceptibility and weather. In humid subtropical vineyards, double
cropping maintains green host tissue across production cycles and can
sustain asexual sporangia beyond the seasonal reset assumed by
oospore-based models. FSIM estimates the first visible symptom-onset
window during the first crop growing season, and FSIM-S assesses the
additional value of field spore observations.

\textbf{RESULTS:} Across six Guangxi site--years, airborne
\emph{Plasmopara viticola} sporangia were detected 9--72 days before the
first visible symptoms. FSIM links cohort-based sporangium production
with dispersal, deposition, zoospore release, infection establishment and
incubation to estimate the symptom-onset window. Validation MAE was 5.67 days for FSIM and
3.83 days for FSIM-S. FSIM-S incorporates an observation-derived relative
spore-pressure field before dispersal and improved retrospective onset
estimation. Regional FSIM-S reconstruction produced mean onset dates of
DOY 195--198, with most estimates between DOY 180 and 210, and indicated
an average of 3.0 potentially avoidable sprays per season (median 2.7)
relative to the fixed-calendar programme.

\textbf{CONCLUSION:} Representing recurrent sporangial inoculum before
symptom appearance addresses a modelling gap in subtropical vineyards.
FSIM-S improves onset estimation and supports process-based timing of
preventive fungicide applications during the first crop growing season.
Regional onset maps and spray-saving estimates support spatially targeted,
risk-based disease management across Guangxi's double-cropping vineyards.

\textbf{Keywords:} Agroclimatic modelling; Airborne sporangia;
Disease-onset estimation; Fungicide timing; Subtropical viticulture

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Grapevine downy mildew, caused by \emph{Plasmopara viticola}, threatens
production in humid subtropical regions, where warm, rainy conditions
coincide with the emergence of susceptible leaves and development of
inflorescences (Koledenkova et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2024). Epidemics
can impair canopy photosynthesis, damage inflorescences and berries, and
cause substantial yield loss (Nogueira Júnior et al., 2019). Disease
control depends heavily on repeated preventive fungicide applications
(Gessler et al., 2011; Caffi et al., 2010). Timing based on calendar
schedules, grower experience or qualitative weather cues may be poorly
aligned with infection risk. Understanding when inoculum can establish
infection is therefore central to protecting susceptible tissue before
symptoms appear. Preventive management based on infection risk
requires joint consideration of inoculum availability, host
susceptibility and environmental suitability (Scholthof, 2007).

Empirical models such as the ``3--10 rule'' and EPI use weather and
phenological indicators to guide control timing (Baldacci, 1947; Strizyk,
1983; Caffi et al., 2007), but they do not resolve the biological sequence
from inoculum availability to symptom appearance. Process-based models
provide a more explicit representation by linking the inoculum source,
spore maturation or production, dispersal, deposition, zoospore release,
infection and incubation (Rossi et al., 2008; Brischetto et al., 2020,
2021). Rossi et al. (2008) used cohorts to represent primary infection from
overwintered oospores, whereas Brischetto et al. (2021) modelled
sporangia produced on lesions and the subsequent infections. Together, these
studies describe the complete sequence of infection processes with greater biological
interpretability than empirical rules, but they begin from distinct
inoculum states: germinating oospores or existing visible lesions.

These initial conditions do not fully represent humid subtropical vineyards.
The long growing season and mild winters support commercial double cropping
in southern China, including Guangxi. The first crop growing season is
typically the summer cropping cycle; pruning and bud forcing after the first
harvest then initiate a second crop growing season in winter. Two harvests
per year can increase annual yield and extend the marketing season (Chen et
al., 2017; Lu et al., 2022). However, successive crops also extend the availability
of green grapevine tissues, allowing the pathogen to persist on living hosts
and replenish airborne sporangia through repeated asexual reproduction
between cropping cycles (Camargo et al., 2019). This continuity contrasts
with the temperate disease cycle, in which resting oospores survive winter
in the absence of susceptible host tissue and germinate to provide the
initial inoculum (Gessler et al., 2011; Vercesi et al., 2010). Predominantly clonal
populations, asexual persistence or limited oospore occurrence have been
reported in subtropical or warm viticultural regions, including São Paulo,
South Africa and most surveyed Western Australian vineyards (Camargo et al.,
2019; Koopman et al., 2007; Killigrew et al., 2005). Asexual sporangia may
therefore be an important source of initial inoculum in Guangxi. Models
initialized from oospores or visible local lesions do not directly represent
this source before symptoms appear, leaving a gap in predicting disease
onset during the first crop growing season.

We developed the \emph{First Seasonal Infection Model} (FSIM) to estimate
the first visible symptom-onset window during the first crop growing
season, when recurrent airborne inoculum is present. Starting from a pool
of asexual inoculum, FSIM represents sporangium production, airborne
dispersal, deposition, zoospore release, infection establishment and
incubation. It combines the
cohort structure of Rossi et al. (2008) with the sporangial processes
described by Brischetto et al. (2021) to estimate the window of first
symptom appearance. FSIM-S denotes the spore-constrained extension of FSIM,
in which field observations of background airborne sporangia provide
information on site--year variation in inoculum availability within the
same mechanistic framework.

The framework was evaluated using assessments of disease onset and airborne
sporangia records from six Guangxi site--years collected during
2022--2024. We assessed whether FSIM and FSIM-S reproduced the timing of
the first visible symptoms during the first crop growing season and whether
the spore-constrained extension improved retrospective symptom-onset
estimation. By linking regional inoculum availability with symptom-onset
timing, the framework provides a basis for scheduling
preventive fungicide applications according to infection risk in Guangxi's
subtropical vineyards.

\section{Materials and methods}\label{materials-and-methods}

\subsection{Study design and overall
workflow}\label{study-design-and-overall-workflow}

This field-based modelling study combined observations from two
commercial vineyards and one experimental orchard in Nanning, Guilin
and Hechi, Guangxi, during 2022--2024. The analysis comprised field
monitoring, development of FSIM and FSIM-S, and retrospective comparison
of disease-onset estimates across six site--years
(\hyperref[_Ref225433957]{Fig 1}). Observations included airborne
sporangia, first confirmed visible downy mildew symptoms, grapevine
phenology and agronomic management records. Site-specific hourly
meteorological records provided the environmental forcing for model
simulations (Hersbach et al., 2020).

FSIM simulated the infection-establishment, incubation and symptom-development
processes during the first crop growing season from weather, phenology and a
recurrent sporangial inoculum pool, and estimated the first visible
symptom-onset window. FSIM-S retained the same process chain and incorporated
an observation-derived relative spore-pressure field. Both models were
calibrated and evaluated with interval-censored symptom-onset observations
under site--year leave-one-out
cross-validation; FSIM-S was then applied to regional onset reconstruction
and spray-management scenario analysis.

\subsection{Field experiments and data
collection}\label{field-experiments-and-data-collection}

\subsubsection{Site description, climate, vineyard
layout}\label{site-description-climate-vineyard-layout}

Field experiments were conducted from 2022 to 2024 in three grape
production orchards in Guangxi, China: Xing'an in Guilin, Du'an in Hechi,
and Jiangnan in Nanning. The orchards were selected to represent common
local production scales and cultivars, and their site characteristics are
summarized in \hyperref[_Ref222580265]{Table 1} (\hyperref[_Ref212629919]{Fig
2a,b}).

The study sites have suitable autumn--winter conditions, with relatively
mild temperatures, high humidity, and recurrent rainfall
(\hyperref[_Ref214271335]{Fig 3}). Orchards surrounding the experimental
sites commonly use a double-cropping production system.

Under this system, vines are pruned and forced again after the first crop
is harvested. The autumn crop then continues to develop through autumn and
is harvested in January of the following year, allowing grape production to
continue through the cool season.

At each vineyard, two management treatments were established to
distinguish natural infection dynamics from management-modified disease
pressure: a traditional treatment (TT), in which fungicides were applied
following local management calendars, and a no-spray control (CK), in
which no fungicides were applied. A completely randomized design was
used, with three replicates per treatment, resulting in six observation
plots per vineyard. Each plot consisted of six uniformly growing vines,
which were used for subsequent phenological and disease monitoring.

\subsubsection{Disease and phenology data
collection}\label{disease-and-phenology-data-collection}

Grapevine phenological development and downy mildew progression were
monitored throughout the growing season following a standardized field
observation protocol. All assessments were conducted in situ by trained
technicians and recorded using a dedicated WeChat-based mobile data
collection system (``Nong Qing Zhu Shou''), which provided structured
interfaces for extended BBCH coding (Lorenz et al., 1995), lesion
annotation, automatic time-stamping, GPS geolocation, and image capture.
Records were uploaded directly to a cloud database, allowing continuous
checks of data completeness and internal consistency.

Observation frequency was adjusted according to developmental stage:
surveys were conducted weekly from BBCH 00 to BBCH 13 (dormancy to three
unfolded leaves), and every two days thereafter as vines entered the
susceptible period for downy mildew. Suspected lesions were photographed
and centrally reviewed; the first confirmed oil spot in any observation
plot defined the first symptom-onset date
(\hyperref[_Ref214184122]{Fig 4b}).

\subsubsection{Airborne sporangia
monitoring}\label{airborne-sporangia-monitoring}

At each vineyard, one passive spore trap (model TPBZ1, Zhejiang Top
Cloud-Agri Technology Co., Ltd., China) was installed adjacent to CK-1,
the fungicide-free control plot, to monitor the airborne dispersal of
\emph{P. viticola} sporangia. The air inlet was positioned approximately
1.5 m above ground level, matching the average grapevine canopy height.
The device sampled sporangia through natural ambient airflow without
powered air suction.

Sporangia were collected on glass slides coated with a thin layer of
Vaseline, which were replaced every two days. Samples were sealed and
transported to the laboratory of the Guangxi Academy of Agricultural
Sciences for quantitative analysis. For microscopic observation, each
slide was rinsed with 300 \(\mu\)L of sterile water, and five 30 \(\mu\)L
subsamples were collected from different depths of the rinse solution to
minimize bias associated with particle sedimentation
(\hyperref[_Ref214187767]{Fig 5b--d}). Subsamples were transferred to
concave glass slides for microscopic observation and imaging.

Images and resulting counts were independently verified by two trained
laboratory technicians before being used in model calibration and
validation (\hyperref[_Ref214187767]{Fig 5c}).

\subsubsection{Meteorological data}\label{meteorological-data}

Meteorological data were retrieved from the Open-Meteo platform
(\href{https://open-meteo.com/}{\ul{https://open-meteo.com/}}), which
provides ERA5 reanalysis products with hourly temporal resolution
(Hersbach et al., 2020). For each experimental orchard, geographic
coordinates were used to extract site-specific time series of air
temperature (T), relative humidity (RH), wind speed (W), dew-point
temperature (T$_d$), and precipitation (R). Vapor pressure deficit (VPD)
was calculated from T and RH following Rossi et al. (2008). Because direct
leaf-wetness observations were unavailable, dew-point depression (DPD) was
used as a leaf-wetness proxy (Huber and Gillespie, 1992). DPD was
calculated as $\mathrm{DPD}=T-T_d$; rainfall or DPD $<2.0$ $^{\circ}$C
indicated wetness, DPD $>3.8$ $^{\circ}$C indicated drying, and
intermediate values retained the previous wet/dry state.

\subsection{Model structure and
refinements}\label{model-structure-and-refinements}

\subsubsection{First Seasonal Infection Model (FSIM): baseline
mechanistic
framework}\label{first-seasonal-infection-model-fsim-baseline-mechanistic-framework}

The First Seasonal Infection Model (FSIM) was developed to simulate the
infection-establishment, incubation, and symptom-development chain
initiated by asexual sporangia during the first crop growing season in
subtropical vineyards.

Because vines in the study region commonly undergo leaf fall during
January--February, FSIM simulations began on 1 February. The FSIM was
constructed through structural integration and process reorganization of
the Rossi primary infection model and secondary infection risk frameworks
(Brischetto et al., 2021; Rossi et al., 2008).

The FSIM process chain, summarized in \hyperref[_Ref_model_concept]{Fig 6},
ultimately outputs the window or time interval in which the first visible
lesion appears. Its components retained from the Rossi model include
zoospore release and survival, infection establishment, incubation, and
symptom expression, which constitute the mechanistic backbone of the
infection-to-symptom chain. The secondary infection risk module was
positioned upstream to describe the production of sporangia, followed by
the infection chain derived from the Rossi model, including zoospore
release and survival, infection establishment, incubation, and symptom
expression. In this restructuring, the probability-accumulation
formulation of the original secondary infection model was reformulated
into a cohort-based representation describing spore production,
accumulation, depletion, and release. Because the inoculum entering the
first-season route was treated as an external input, transfer to the
airborne state was wind-driven. Rainfall provided the deposition
opportunity and the wet conditions required by subsequent survival and
release. A heavy-rain wash-off adjustment was included because intense
rainfall can remove spores from the air column and reduce long-range
dispersal (Gobbin et al., 2005).

The cohort-based dispersal--deposition mechanism was defined as follows.
The attached sporangia pool \(A_{t}\) was updated before transfer. When
\(A_{t}\) exceeded the minimum threshold \(A_{\min}\) and wind speed
\(W_{t}\) exceeded \(W_{\min}\), the pool was transferred to the airborne
state:

\begin{equation}
B_{t_{0}} = A_{t_{0}},\ A_{t_{0}} \rightarrow 0
\end{equation}

where \(t_{0}\) denotes the wind-transfer time and \(B_{t_{0}}\) the
initial airborne sporangia amount. Detached-state loss started at
\(t_{0}\) and was updated hourly while the cohort remained airborne:

\begin{equation}
B_{t+1}=B_{t_{0}}\max\!\left(1-\sum_{u=t_{0}}^{t}\lambda_{u},0\right),
\qquad t \geq t_{0},
\end{equation}

where \(\lambda_{t}\) is the weather-dependent detached-state loss rate.
An effective deposition event occurred only when the surviving airborne
amount was positive and rainfall was sufficient for deposition but below
the wash-off threshold:

\begin{equation}
D_{t_d}=B_{t_d^-}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left(R_{\mathrm{dep}}\leq R_{t_d}<R_{\mathrm{wash}}\right)\,
\mathbf{1}\!\left(B_{t_d^-}>B_{\min}\right),
\end{equation}

where \(t_d\) is the deposition time, \(R_{\mathrm{dep}}\) is the
effective-deposition threshold, and \(R_{\mathrm{wash}}\) is the
heavy-rain wash-off threshold. Rain below \(R_{\mathrm{dep}}\) leaves the
cohort airborne, whereas rain at or above \(R_{\mathrm{wash}}\) removes it
before effective deposition. Thus, deposition can contribute to infection
only while the detached cohort remains within its survival period.

Field monitoring indicated that airborne pathogen structures captured
prior to the appearance of visible lesions were predominantly sporangia
(\hyperref[_Ref214187767]{Fig 5d}), providing observational support for
the sporulation, dispersal, and deposition modules included in the FSIM.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Spore-constrained refinement of the
\emph{First Seasonal Infection Model}
(FSIM-S)}{Spore-constrained refinement of the First Seasonal Infection Model (FSIM-S)}}\label{spore-constrained-refinement-of-the-first-seasonal-infection-model-fsim-s}

FSIM-S was defined as an observation-constrained extension of FSIM. It
retained the FSIM sequence of weather-triggered sporangium production,
wind-gated transfer, rainfall-mediated deposition, detached survival,
infection establishment, incubation, and symptom expression. The extension
used airborne sporangium observations to constrain inoculum availability and
used intervals with numeric zero counts to suppress weather-generated cohorts
when no sporangia were captured. Missing entries were treated as missing and
did not activate this suppression rule.

Airborne sporangia were recorded as accumulated counts over collection
intervals. Let \(t_j\) denote the \(j\)th non-missing collection date and
\(C_j\) its count. For an interval of duration
\(\Delta_j=(t_j-t_{j-1})/\mathrm{d}\), the corresponding daily rate was
calculated as

\begin{equation}
r(d)=\frac{C_j}{\Delta_j},\qquad t_{j-1}<d\leq t_j.
\end{equation}

The daily rates were standardized using the mean \(\overline{r}_{M}\) and
standard deviation \(s_{M}\) of the measured exposure days \(M\), smoothed
with a three-day centered mean, and centered on the reference level
\(L=0.5\). For day \(d\), \(W_d=\{d-1,d,d+1\}\cap\mathcal{D}\), where
\(\mathcal{D}\) denotes the weather calendar, the resulting centered
spore-pressure signal was

\begin{equation}
P(d)=\frac{1}{\lvert W_d\rvert}\sum_{u\in W_d}
\frac{r(u)-\overline{r}_{M}}{s_{M}}-L,\qquad L=0.5.
\end{equation}

The daily value \(P(d_t)\) was assigned to each hourly timestamp \(t\) on
the corresponding local calendar day. At the daily sporangium-availability
time (08:00), the positive part of this signal was converted to an external
background dose. The total dose entering the attached sporangium pool was
defined as

\begin{equation}
D^{\mathrm{obs}}_{t}=\beta_{\mathrm{sp}}\max\{P(d_t),0\},\qquad
D^{\mathrm{total}}_{t}=G(d_t)D^{\mathrm{weather}}_{t}+D^{\mathrm{obs}}_{t}.
\end{equation}

Here, (G(d)=0) for days covered by an interval with a numeric zero
count and (G(d)=1) otherwise; missing observations therefore do not
suppress weather-generated cohorts.

The combined dose then entered the FSIM attached-sporangium pool and passed
through the same wind transfer, rain-mediated deposition, survival,
infection-establishment, incubation, and symptom-development processes.
Thus, FSIM-S constrains inoculum supply before dispersal while retaining the
FSIM downstream process relations. The conceptual structure of the baseline
and spore-constrained models is summarized in
\hyperref[_Ref_model_concept]{Fig 6}.

\subsection{Parameterization and sensitivity
analysis}\label{parameterization-and-sensitivity-analysis}

Global parameter influence was quantified for the sporangia-process route
using variance-based Sobol sensitivity analysis (Sobol, 2001; Hui et al.,
2022; Iooss and Lemaître, 2015). Model parameters were sampled according
to the implementation settings. The
observation-derived spore-pressure field and pressure-specific coefficients
were held fixed, and the sensitivity analysis was used for parameter
screening rather than calibration.

For each base sample size \(N\) (256, 512, and 1024), a Sobol sequence with
first-, total- and second-order indices was generated using SALib. The same
site--year inputs, observed phenology and interval-based onset metric were
used for every design. First-order \(S_{1}\), total-order \(S_{T}\), and
second-order \(S_{2}\) indices were estimated with 100 bootstrap resamples
and 95\% confidence intervals. Missing symptom-onset windows were retained in the
coverage outputs and assigned the prespecified 60-day penalty only for
penalized MAE; outputs with nonfinite values or zero variance were not
decomposed. Stability was assessed by comparing indices and parameter
rankings across the three values of \(N\), rather than by treating larger
designs as additional biological replicates. No parameter configuration was
selected from the sensitivity analysis.

\subsection{Model calibration and
evaluation}\label{model-calibration-and-evaluation}

To assess the temporal predictive capability for first visible symptom
onset and the applicability of the
\emph{First Seasonal Infection Model} (FSIM) and its spore-constrained
extension (FSIM-S) under humid subtropical vineyard conditions, parameter
calibration and model evaluation were conducted within a site--year-based
cross-validation framework. The two models used the same weather and
phenology inputs and the same interval-based symptom-onset metric; FSIM-S additionally
used the fixed interval-rate, z-score spore-pressure field and the calibrated
coefficient \(\beta_{\mathrm{sp}}\).

\subsubsection{Calibration strategy}\label{calibration-strategy}

Calibration used a site--year leave-one-out design. In each of the
six outer folds, one site--year was held out and the remaining five were used
for calibration. Six shared FSIM process parameters were first fitted with a
bounded finite coarse-to-fine coordinate search: one-at-a-time screening on
the prescribed coarse grids was followed by coarse coordinate descent and
local fine coordinate descent. Fine candidates were generated at the
parameter-specific step size within two steps on either side of the current
value. The calibrated parameters were the infection-establishment degree-hour threshold,
minimum wind speed, effective deposition rainfall threshold, sporulation
relative-humidity threshold, minimum moist-hours requirement, and heavy-rain
wash-off threshold.

After the FSIM parameter vector was fixed, \(\beta_{\mathrm{sp}}\) was
calibrated on the same five training site--years. The coefficient was bounded
to \([0,1]\) and selected by a coarse grid followed by local refinement with a
step of 0.05. Candidate configurations were ranked by penalized MAE; ties
were resolved by preferring non-positive mean signed error, then the smallest
absolute signed error, and finally the configuration closest to the prior
values.

\subsubsection{Performance metrics}\label{performance-metrics}

Visible disease-onset observations were interval-censored by the survey schedule.
For site--year \(i\), let \(t_i^{-}\) be the last disease-negative survey
date and \(t_i^{+}\) the first disease-positive survey date. The observed
onset window was therefore

\begin{equation}
O_i=[t_i^{-},t_i^{+}],\qquad t_i^{-}=t_i^{+}-3\,\mathrm{d}.
\end{equation}

For each model, \(\widehat{O}_i=[\widehat{t}_i^{-},\widehat{t}_i^{+}]\)
was defined as the first complete simulated symptom-onset window. The signed
interval distance, expressed in days, was

\begin{equation}
e_i=\begin{cases}
0, & \widehat{t}_i^{-}\leq t_i^{+}\ \mathrm{and}\ \widehat{t}_i^{+}\geq t_i^{-},\\
\widehat{t}_i^{-}-t_i^{+}, & \widehat{t}_i^{-}>t_i^{+},\\
\widehat{t}_i^{+}-t_i^{-}, & \widehat{t}_i^{+}<t_i^{-}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Thus, overlapping windows had zero error, whereas non-overlapping windows
were assigned the signed distance between their nearest boundaries. For the
set \(C\) of site--years with a complete predicted window, interval-based
MAE and RMSE were calculated as

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{i\in C}|e_i|,\qquad
\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{i\in C}e_i^{2}}.
\end{equation}

If no complete symptom-onset window was simulated, the site--year received a fixed
60-day penalty in the calibration objective. The corresponding penalized MAE
was \(\{\sum_{i\in C}|e_i|+(n-|C|)\times60\,\mathrm{d}\}/n\), where \(n\)
is the total number of site--years. Complete-window MAE, RMSE, signed mean
error, and window coverage were retained for evaluation.

\subsubsection{Cross-validation and robustness
assessment}\label{cross-validation-and-robustness-assessment}

Each held-out site--year was evaluated once with the calibrated FSIM
parameter vector and once with the same vector augmented by the calibrated
\(\beta_{\mathrm{sp}}\) and the spore-pressure dose. No observations from the
held-out site--year were used during either calibration stage. Cross-validation
summaries were based on the held-out interval errors and reported complete
window coverage together with interval MAE, RMSE, and signed mean error for
FSIM and FSIM-S.

\subsubsection{Selection of global parameter
configuration}\label{selection-of-global-parameter-configuration}

To obtain a single deterministic model configuration for site--year
simulations, the fold-specific best parameter sets (those achieving the
lowest validation MAE within each fold) were evaluated on the full
dataset. The configuration yielding the lowest overall MAE was selected
as the final global parameter set. Direct averaging of parameters across
folds was avoided, as it led to substantial error inflation due to the
non-convex nature of the objective function and the presence of multiple
near-optimal parameter regions.


\subsection{Regional simulation and disease-management scenario
analysis}\label{regional-simulation-and-disease-management-scenario-analysis}

To evaluate the applicability of the FSIM-S model at the regional scale
and its potential value for risk-based disease management, regional
simulations were conducted across Guangxi Province for the period
2000--2024. Given the limited spatial coverage of field observations and
the lack of a spatially explicit vineyard census layer, the regional
analysis was framed as a scenario-based assessment rather than as a
complete mapping of actual vineyard distribution. Three cultivar
scenarios were constructed to ensure comparability across the region,
assuming that vineyards were uniformly planted with Wink, Yeniang No. 2,
or Kyoho cultivars, respectively. The model was driven by the hourly
meteorological dataset described previously (\hyperref[_Ref214271335]{Fig
3}).

Considering that growers in the region commonly adopt similar preventive
spraying schedules through peer-to-peer technical exchange, chemical
disease management was represented using a schedule derived from the 2022
Du'an (Hechi) experimental vineyard (\hyperref[_Ref214272651]{Fig 7})
(Chavas and Nauges, 2020; Huffman, 2020; Norton and Alwang, 2020). The
potential to avoid early preventive treatments was evaluated relative to
this baseline schedule, whereby any preventive fungicide application
conducted more than seven days before the simulated first symptom onset
was classified as potentially avoidable. The seven-day buffer was used to
remain consistent with the protection window commonly expected for
vineyard fungicide applications and to avoid classifying near-risk sprays
as redundant. Based on this criterion, the multi-year average number of
potentially avoidable early applications and the corresponding reduction
proportion were calculated.

\subsubsection{Phenology simulation}
\label{phenology-simulation}

To provide phenological inputs for regional simulations, we trained a
temperature-driven grapevine phenology model from the historical
phenology observations. The model follows the growing degree day (GDD)
approach commonly used for grapevine phenology (Wang et al., 2020). For
each day \(d\), daily GDD was calculated as

\begin{equation}
GDD_d = \max(\overline{T}_d - T_b, 0),
\end{equation}

where \(\overline{T}_d\) is the daily mean air temperature and \(T_b\) is
the base temperature. Starting on 1 January, thermal time was accumulated
between consecutive stages as

\begin{equation}
C_s(t) = \sum_{d=t_{s-1}+1}^{t} GDD_d,
\qquad
t_s = \min\{t:C_s(t) \geq H_s\},
\end{equation}

where \(t_s\) is the date of stage \(s\) and \(H_s\) is its calibrated
thermal requirement. The model generated dates for BBCH09, BBCH53, BBCH65,
and BBCH81; BBCH53 was derived from the interval between BBCH09 and BBCH65
using the calibrated auxiliary-stage rule. These dates were used as
phenological inputs to the regional FSIM-S simulations.

\subsubsection{Reconstruction of spore pressure
fields}\label{reconstruction-of-spore-pressure-fields}

Because continuous airborne spore observations were unavailable for the
historical period (2000--2024), while the FSIM-S model requires inoculum
pressure information, a meteorology-driven spore-pressure reconstruction
module was developed. Field spore monitoring data collected during
2022--2024 were used for parameter calibration, after which spatially
explicit spore-pressure time series were reconstructed to drive historical
simulations.

The module follows a ``potential inoculum reservoir--pressure index''
dynamic framework, in which meteorological effects on spore production and
loss are represented as source and loss terms:

\begin{equation}
A_{t} = \max\left(0,\rho A_{t - 1} + Source_{t} - Loss_{t}\right)
\end{equation}

where \(A_{t}\) denotes the latent inoculum state. After logarithmic
normalization and smoothing, the resulting daily spore-pressure index
was used to construct the forcing field for historical FSIM-S simulations.

To ensure consistency with the disease process model, meteorological
drivers used in pressure reconstruction shared the same physical
interpretation as those controlling infection processes in FSIM-S. The
pressure module employed a simplified inoculum-reservoir dynamic that was
independently calibrated, enabling forcing input under conditions lacking
long-term spore observations. Calibration was performed using Bayesian
optimization with cross-validation, and the resulting parameters were
applied to reconstruct gridded spore-pressure fields for 2000--2024,
which were subsequently coupled to FSIM-S through the spore-pressure dose
pathway.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

This study addressed a key agronomic challenge in subtropical
viticulture: improving early-season grapevine downy mildew management
when viable inoculum may be present before weather and crop conditions
become jointly favourable for infection establishment. By reconstructing Rossi's
primary and secondary infection formulations into a First Seasonal
Infection Model (FSIM), and then incorporating observation-based
spore-pressure dynamics (FSIM-S), the study linked on-farm inoculum
monitoring with agroclimatic modelling of symptom onset.

Across multiple site--year datasets from Guangxi, China, FSIM-S
improved prediction accuracy and stability compared with FSIM while
retaining the dominant influence of meteorological drivers. These
results support the use of FSIM-S as a region-adapted mechanistic
framework for field-based downy mildew decision support in subtropical
vineyards, rather than as an empirical model fitted only to symptom
dates.

\subsection{Inoculum dynamics and the drivers of first seasonal symptom
onset}\label{inoculum-dynamics-and-the-drivers-of-first-seasonal-symptom-onset}

In temperate systems, seasonal epidemics are typically initiated by
overwintering oospores after a distinct climatic break. In contrast, the
continuous presence of airborne sporangia during the early growing
season (\hyperref[_Ref214187767]{Fig 5}, \hyperref[_Ref212492136]{Fig
8}) indicates that inoculum in the studied subtropical vineyards is not
restricted to a discrete primary infection event. This distinction has
direct implications for model structure, because frameworks centred on
oospore-driven primary infection may not fully represent systems in
which asexual inoculum is recurrently available.

Despite this continuous inoculum presence, the timing of first visible
symptom onset varied substantially across sites and years
(\hyperref[_Ref212492136]{Fig 8}). This variability is consistent with
interannual differences in early-season meteorological conditions.
Temperature, relative humidity, and leaf wetness duration regulate
infection establishment (Rossi and Caffi, 2012; Yi et al., 2019; Yu et
al., 2022), whereas sustained rainfall can promote sporangial
production, release and dispersal from vineyard inoculum sources
(Orlandini et al., 2008). Accordingly, climate variability affects
disease onset through both infection-establishment suitability and inoculum
dynamics, explaining why first symptom onset cannot be inferred from weather
suitability alone.

The temporal decoupling between sporangia detection and symptom onset
provides further evidence for this interpretation. Across all datasets,
the interval between first airborne sporangia detection and subsequent
symptom appearance ranged from 9 to 72 days
(\hyperref[_Ref212492136]{Fig 8}), indicating that the presence of
inoculum is necessary but insufficient for infection establishment and
subsequent symptom expression. Infection establishment depends also on host
phenological susceptibility
(Hui et al., 2022) and on sufficient inoculum pressure to support
effective deposition and infection establishment (Scholthof, 2007; Van der Plank,
2013; Zhang et al., 2025). This decoupling indicates that inoculum
pressure should be represented as a dynamic constraint on infection
triggering, rather than treated as a static prerequisite.

\subsection{Model enhancement}\label{model-enhancement}

The incorporation of spore-pressure dynamics into FSIM-S improved
predictive performance relative to the baseline FSIM, with validation
MAE reduced from 5.67 to 3.83 days (32.4\% reduction)
(\hyperref[fig:fsim-error-metrics]{Fig 10}). The improvement suggests that first symptom onset tends to
occur within comparatively narrow windows in which environmental
suitability coincides with sufficient pathogen pressure, consistent with
the disease triangle concept (Van der Plank, 2013). The model therefore
adds process information beyond weather-only infection suitability while
retaining direct biological interpretability.

City--year errors showed the largest error magnitude in Duan 2022 (14 days;
error = -14 days) for
both models, whereas Duan 2024 had zero error under both models and Guilin
2022 had zero error under FSIM-S (\hyperref[fig:fsim-site-year-errors]{Fig
11}). FSIM-S reduced the absolute error in Duan 2023, Mingyang 2023, and
Guilin 2022, while the remaining city--years were unchanged.

Overall, FSIM provides a mechanistic representation of first seasonal
infection-to-symptom processes in subtropical vineyards where recurrent sporangia
production is a major inoculum source. FSIM-S further integrates an
observation-derived spore-pressure field, improving responsiveness to
symptom-onset timing variability while preserving the core biological
processes inherited from mechanistic infection modelling. This balance
between biological structure and field observation is important for
developing transferable early-warning tools under variable subtropical
conditions.

\subsection{Implications for inoculum-informed preventive
timing}\label{implications-for-inoculum-informed-preventive-timing}

Airborne inoculum monitoring can complement weather-based assessment by
indicating the background pathogen pressure under which favourable
infection conditions occur. FSIM-S connects these observations with a
mechanistic sequence, providing a basis for investigating the timing
of early-season risk. The evaluated endpoint is visible symptom onset,
whereas preventive protection concerns the earlier infection-risk
period. Reconstructed symptom-onset timing therefore requires prospective
evaluation with inputs available at the time of a decision before they
can be translated into treatment recommendations. Demonstrating reduced
fungicide use with maintained disease control would additionally require
field comparisons of conventional, model-guided and untreated programmes.

\subsection{Agronomic implications for climate-smart vineyard disease
management}

The regional simulations provide an agronomic interpretation of when
model-guided protection would be most relevant across Guangxi. FSIM-S
  predicted a mean first symptom-onset date between DOY 195 and 198, with a
high-frequency window spanning DOY 180--210
(\hyperref[_Ref223011563]{Fig 12}). This period overlaps with the regional
flowering--fruit-set stage, when young tissues are particularly susceptible
(Wang et al., 2020; Cheng et al., 2022), and with the Meiyu season,
characterized by warm, humid conditions and intermittent rainfall (Song et
al., 2021; Tian et al., 2022). Thus, the regional results are not only
spatial reconstructions of symptom-onset timing; they also identify the
seasonal context in which downy mildew decisions are most consequential for
vineyard production.

Within this context, the management implication of FSIM-S is to translate
infection biology and agroclimatic variability into a time-explicit risk
signal. This signal can support decisions on early scouting, spore
monitoring intensity, protection timing and chemical intervention. When
model outputs were compared with conventional calendar-based programmes,
the model-guided scenario avoided an average of approximately three early
preventive sprays per season (range: 2--5), equivalent to a 20--50\%
reduction in the simulated early-season programme (Gisi and Sierotzki,
2008; Rossi et al., 2008; Rossin et al., 2015; Koledenkova et al., 2022).
This result should be interpreted as a timing benefit rather than as a
generic fungicide-reduction claim: the model delays pre-risk sprays while
retaining protection near the simulated symptom-onset window.

The climate-smart value of this framework therefore lies in shifting
subtropical downy mildew management from fixed calendar schedules toward
risk-informed decision support. FSIM-S does not imply that early protection
can be omitted; rather, it identifies periods when host susceptibility,
spore-pressure dynamics and agroclimatic suitability converge. Such
information can improve the biological justification of interventions,
increase pesticide-use efficiency, and support more sustainable viticulture
under humid subtropical conditions (Russell, 1995; Zubrod et al., 2019;
Zhang et al., 2020).

\subsection{Limitations and future
directions}\label{limitations-and-future-directions}

First, model parameterization used observations from two commercial
vineyards and one experimental orchard over three growing seasons. This
provides multi-site and multi-year evidence under realistic subtropical
production conditions, while additional observations across more
regions, cultivars and management systems would further broaden the
calibration domain. The 2022 Guilin season, for example, provided a
useful contrast because unusually low early-season humidity and
irregular rainfall (\hyperref[_Ref214271335]{Fig 3}) may have delayed
or suppressed infection establishment. Such contrasting cases are
valuable for testing model behaviour, but wider datasets would support
more explicit cultivar- or region-specific parameterization (Bellin et
al., 2009; Gessler et al., 2011; Koledenkova et al., 2022).

Second, FSIM-S currently uses a deterministic mechanistic structure to
describe the coupling between meteorological conditions and
spore-pressure dynamics. This formulation favours stability,
interpretability and transparent biological assumptions. Future probabilistic extensions could complement this structure
by quantifying uncertainty associated with rainfall variability, spore
sampling error and fine-scale atmospheric heterogeneity, particularly
when the model is applied to operational warning systems or climate
scenario analysis.

Third, the regional analysis was designed as a scenario-based assessment
rather than a vineyard-by-vineyard operational map. It therefore used
simplified assumptions about vineyard distribution, canopy structure and
cultivar allocation. These assumptions are appropriate for evaluating broad
regional patterns and management windows, but finer-scale implementation
would benefit from vineyard land-use layers, cultivar distribution data,
remote-sensing information and canopy microclimate observations (Tesic et
al., 2007; Perria et al., 2022). Such additions would extend the framework
from regional risk assessment toward more spatially resolved
disease-management support.

Finally, the six site--years provide a limited basis for geographical
or cultivar transfer because site and cultivar are confounded. Further
independent observations and prospective evaluation are needed to
establish the applicability of the framework beyond these vineyards.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

FSIM links established cohort-based and airborne-sporangium processes
under a recurrent-airborne-inoculum premise to reconstruct first visible
downy mildew symptom onset in subtropical vineyards. FSIM-S incorporates
field-observed spore pressure within the same process chain, enabling
assessment of its incremental information for symptom-onset estimation.
Multi-year and multi-site validation demonstrated that FSIM-S reproduced
first visible symptom-onset timing with a mean absolute error of 6.3 days and
a root mean square error of 6.5 days. Long-term regional simulations
(2000--2024) captured spatial patterns of symptom onset and indicated
that model-guided timing could avoid 2--5 early preventive sprays per season
compared with a conventional calendar-based programme. Overall, the
framework connects inoculum monitoring with infection biology and provides
a basis for climate-smart, model-guided disease management under subtropical
conditions.
